## 1. What Is It? (Theory)

### The Problem at Scale

You are running a service used by millions of people. How do you answer the question: **"Is the service working?"**

At 10 users, you know intuitively. At 10 million users, "working" stops being a binary. At any given moment, 0.01% of requests are failing. Is that acceptable? Is it a crisis? It depends — but depends on *what*?

This is the problem SLIs, SLOs, and SLAs solve: they give teams a **shared, quantitative language for reliability**. Without them, you get:

- Engineering and business in endless arguments about whether something is "good enough"
- On-call engineers paged at 3am for things that don't matter to users
- Feature development frozen during incidents that are actually within normal operating parameters
- Customers surprised by outages that engineering saw coming but had no escalation framework for

### The Three Concepts

**SLI — Service Level Indicator**: A *measurement*. A specific metric that reflects the user experience. Always expressed as a ratio: `good events / total events`.

**SLO — Service Level Objective**: A *target* applied to an SLI over a time window. "We want 99.9% of requests to succeed over the rolling 28-day window." An SLO is an internal engineering contract.

**SLA — Service Level Agreement**: A *contract* with consequences. If the SLO is broken in a way that violates the SLA, compensation is owed — credits, refunds, or penalties. An SLA is a business/legal document.

**Error Budget**: Derived directly from the SLO. If your SLO is 99.9%, your error budget is 0.1%. Over 28 days with 1 million requests, that is exactly 1,000 allowed failures. The error budget is the *operating space* where teams can ship changes without violating the SLO.

### Real-World Analogy

Think of it like aircraft maintenance.

- The **SLI** is the altimeter reading — a precise instrument measurement.
- The **SLO** is the minimum safe altitude — "we must stay above 10,000 feet at all times."
- The **SLA** is the FAA regulation — "if you go below 8,000 feet in controlled airspace, you face penalties."
- The **error budget** is the buffer between 10,000 feet (where you aim to be) and 8,000 feet (where the law kicks in). You have 2,000 feet of room to maneuver, descend for landing, or hit turbulence without breaking the law.

Pilots do not aim to fly *exactly* at 8,000 feet. Engineers should not aim to hit exactly 99.9%. You set an internal SLO tighter than the SLA so you have a safety margin.

### Concrete Example with Numbers

**Service**: Payment processing API (like Stripe)
**SLI**: `(HTTP 2xx responses) / (total HTTP responses)` measured over every 1-minute window, rolled into a 28-day ratio
**SLO**: 99.95% of requests must succeed over a rolling 28-day window
**SLA**: If availability drops below 99.9% in any calendar month, customers receive 10% service credit
**Error budget at 99.95%**: 0.05% of requests may fail
- With 10 million requests/day → 300 million requests/28 days → **150,000 allowed failures** total
- That is 5,357 allowed failures per day, or ~223 per hour

If a deploy causes 500 failures in one minute, you just burned through 2.2 hours of budget in 60 seconds. That is a meaningful signal — not necessarily an outage, but a hard conversation about whether to roll back.

## 2. How It Actually Works (Deep Mechanics)

### Step-by-Step Internal Mechanics

1. **Instrument your service** to emit telemetry. Every request records: timestamp, response time, status code, whether it was "good" by your SLI definition.

2. **Define the SLI precisely** in code/config. "Good" must be unambiguous: a 200 response under 300ms might be good; a 200 response at 5,000ms might not be, depending on your latency SLI.

3. **Aggregate into a rolling window**. Most teams use 28-day rolling windows, not calendar months. Rolling windows avoid the "just reset on the 1st" problem where you can blow your budget on the 2nd and have nothing left for the rest of the month.

4. **Calculate error budget remaining** as a real-time metric: `(current_error_budget_used / total_error_budget) * 100 = % consumed`.

5. **Alert on error budget burn rate**, not on SLO breach. This is the key insight from Google's SRE book: if you only alert when you breach the SLO, you find out too late. Instead, alert when you are *burning budget faster than sustainable*. A burn rate of 1x means you will exhaust the budget at exactly the window boundary. A burn rate of 14.4x means you will exhaust a 30-day budget in 2 days.

6. **Enforce the error budget policy**: teams agree in advance what happens at different burn levels (see Section 8 for thresholds).

7. **Track SLA compliance** separately from SLO compliance. Your SLO should be tighter than your SLA by at least a 5-10% margin of the nines.

### Key Algorithms and Data Structures

**Multi-window burn rate alerting** (from Google's SRE Workbook, Chapter 5):

The recommended alerting strategy uses two time windows simultaneously to avoid both false positives (short spikes) and slow-burn misses:

```
Alert fires when:
  burn_rate(1h) > threshold_fast   AND
  burn_rate(6h) > threshold_slow
```

| Alert Severity | Fast Window | Fast Threshold | Slow Window | Slow Threshold | Budget consumed if fires |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Page (P1) | 1h | 14.4x | 5m | 14.4x | ~2% in 1h |
| Page (P2) | 6h | 6x | 30m | 6x | ~5% in 6h |
| Ticket (P3) | 1d | 3x | 6h | 3x | ~10% in 1d |
| Warning only | 3d | 1x | — | — | Full depletion on track |

**SLI ratio formula:**

```
SLI = good_events(window) / valid_events(window)

where:
  good_events   = requests that met the definition of "good"
  valid_events  = all requests EXCLUDING those deemed invalid
                  (e.g., exclude requests from known load tests,
                   health checks, or explicitly malformed requests
                   that indicate client bugs, not service bugs)
```

**Rolling window implementation** — use a circular buffer or sliding window in your time-series DB. Prometheus `rate()` and `increase()` functions handle this. For a 28-day SLO:

```promql
# Error rate over 28 days
1 - (
  sum(rate(http_requests_total{status=~"5.."}[28d]))
  /
  sum(rate(http_requests_total[28d]))
)
```

### Misconceptions

**❌ Misconception**: 100% uptime is the goal. If you're not at 100%, you're failing.

**✅ Reality**: 100% uptime is unachievable and undesirable. Every deployment, every scaling event, every schema migration burns a tiny amount of budget. A 100% SLO means you can never deploy. The SRE book explicitly states that an SLO less than 100% "acknowledges that perfect reliability is not achievable." You choose a target that balances user experience with engineering velocity.

---

**❌ Misconception**: SLAs and SLOs are the same thing.

**✅ Reality**: They operate at completely different layers. An SLO is an internal technical target. An SLA is a legal contract. Most services have SLOs without SLAs (internal tools, private APIs). Many SLAs are aspirational marketing documents that never get enforced because the measurement methodology is vague. Google Cloud's own SLAs typically trail their internal SLOs by one or more nines.

---

**❌ Misconception**: More nines is always better. Aim for 99.999%.

**✅ Reality**: Five nines means ~5 minutes of downtime per year. Achieving this requires massive investment in redundancy, no-downtime deployments, multi-region failover, and near-instant incident response. For an internal dashboard, this cost is absurd. For a pacemaker firmware update service, it is non-negotiable. Match the SLO to the user pain, not to prestige.

---

**❌ Misconception**: SLI = availability. Just measure uptime.

**✅ Reality**: Uptime (is the server running?) is a terrible proxy for user experience. A server can be "up" while returning 503s to 30% of requests, or responding in 8 seconds to every request. SLIs must be *user-facing*. The four canonical SLI categories (from Google SRE) are: **availability**, **latency**, **throughput**, and **quality/correctness**. A payments API needs all four.

## 3. Real-World Architecture Examples

### Google

**Scale**: Google Search handles ~8.5 billion queries/day. Gmail has 1.8 billion users. Google Cloud serves millions of business customers.

**What they built**: The entire SLI/SLO/SLA/error budget framework was codified at Google. The SRE team at Google invented this language in the mid-2000s, first published in the SRE Book (2016) and SRE Workbook (2018). These are not aspirational practices — they are how Google operates production today.

**Decision they made**: Google separates SLOs (internal engineering targets) from SLAs (customer contracts) by design. Their internal SLOs are tighter than published SLAs by at least one nine. Google Cloud Storage publishes an SLA of 99.9% for multi-region. The internal SLO is 99.95%. This buffer means the SRE team is never surprised by an SLA violation — they have always already been paged and responding before the contractual threshold is breached.

**Error budget policy at Google**: If a service exhausts its error budget in a 28-day window, all feature development stops and the entire team works on reliability until the budget recovers. This is enforced, not advisory. Product managers are stakeholders in the error budget because their feature launches cost budget.

**Key insight from the SRE Workbook**: Google found that alerting on SLO breach was too late. They switched entirely to **multi-window burn rate alerts**. A 14.4x burn rate means the 30-day budget depletes in 2 days. That fires a page. A 1x burn rate fires a ticket — no page, just a flag to check in on the weekly review.

### Netflix

**Scale**: 270+ million subscribers, 700+ microservices, streaming to 190+ countries.

**What they got wrong first**: Early Netflix (2008-2010) operated with implicit availability targets and reactive on-call. Engineers were paged when customers complained on Twitter. There was no formal error budget concept. Every incident felt like a crisis regardless of severity.

**What they corrected**: Netflix adopted a formal SLO framework per service by 2014. Critically, they also invented **Chaos Engineering** (Chaos Monkey, later the Simian Army) as the mechanism to *test* their SLOs. The logic: if your SLO says 99.9% availability, you should be able to randomly kill a production server and still meet it. Chaos Monkey does exactly that during business hours. If your SLO is violated by Chaos Monkey, you learn about it now (controlled, business hours, engineers awake) rather than during an actual failure.

**The December 2012 incident**: Netflix's streaming service experienced failures on Christmas Eve 2012. Traditional monitoring showed "everything green" at first because the alerting was tuned to absolute failure, not to the rate of increase in errors. Their SLO tracking showed that the 99.9% monthly availability SLO was burning through its error budget in ~6 hours. This incident directly motivated the adoption of burn-rate alerting.

**SLO structure at Netflix**: Netflix ties SLOs to their dependency graph. A service's SLO is calculated not just from its own metrics but from the aggregate of its upstream/downstream dependencies. A streaming API that calls 8 microservices needs a composite SLO that accounts for partial degradation — the site can still work if the recommendation service is slow, but cannot work if the DRM service is unavailable.

**Source**: Netflix TechBlog, "How Netflix Ensures Highly-Reliable Online Stateful Systems" (InfoQ, 2023).

### Stripe

**Scale**: 300+ million transactions during peak (Black Friday/Cyber Monday), 99.999% uptime target during peak periods.

**What they got wrong first**: The famous Stripe latency incident (documented across engineering post-mortems) showed that meeting the SLA numerically does not mean users are happy. During a degraded period, traditional uptime metrics read green (the server was responding), error rates stayed under 1% (the SLA threshold), but latency had spiked from ~100ms to ~4,000ms. The system was "up" by contractual definition but unusable in practice.

**Root cause of the gap**: Their SLI was defined only as availability (2xx response rate). They had no latency SLI in the SLA. The contract had a gap. Internal engineers knew latency was broken — external SLO monitoring showed nothing.

**What they corrected**: Stripe added latency as a first-class SLI alongside availability. They now track: `p50`, `p95`, `p99`, and `p99.9` latency independently. Their public status page and SLA now include latency degradation as a named service event, not just outages.

**Target**: Stripe publicly states APIs should be unavailable for no more than ~24 seconds in a 28-day window at 99.999% — that is the budget. Any latency above their threshold for a sustained period counts against the same budget.

**Key lesson**: Every SLI gap is a future outage you will not detect. Define SLIs that reflect how users actually experience the service, not what is easy to measure.

### Cloudflare

**Scale**: 20+ million Internet properties, 280+ data centers, handling trillions of DNS queries daily.

**What they got wrong**: The November 2023 control plane outage lasted from November 2 at 11:44 UTC to November 4 at 04:25 UTC — over 40 hours. The control plane (customer dashboard, API) and analytics (logging, reporting) were affected. Notably, *traffic continued flowing* through Cloudflare's network during this period. The data plane was healthy. This illustrates a critical SLO design question: what is your service's SLI actually measuring — data plane availability, control plane availability, or both?

**Response**: Cloudflare launched "Code Orange: Fail Small" — a formal resilience program that includes explicit SLOs for both data plane and control plane, with separate error budgets for each. Control plane downtime does not consume data plane error budget. This separation is architecturally important: it prevents one failure domain from polluting the SLO of another.

**Key lesson**: Define SLIs and SLOs *per functional boundary*, not per service. A service can have multiple SLOs for different user journeys, each with its own error budget.

### Key Design Questions Before Starting

1. **What does "good" mean for your users?** A search result in 5 seconds is not "good" even if the HTTP status is 200. Define goodness from the user perspective before picking metrics.

2. **What is the user journey?** Payment checkout, video playback, and background batch jobs have very different SLI requirements. Never define a single SLO for a service that has multiple user journeys.

3. **What window length?** 28-day rolling is standard for most services. Calendar month causes "budget reset gaming" at month boundaries. Shorter windows (7-day) are appropriate for services with fast release cycles. Longer windows (90-day) are appropriate for compliance-heavy services.

4. **Where does measurement happen?** Measure as close to the user as possible. Server-side metrics undercount failures (network errors before the request reaches your server). Use synthetic monitoring (probers that simulate user requests from multiple regions) alongside server-side metrics.

5. **What is excluded from the SLI?** Scheduled maintenance? Known load test traffic? Requests with malformed auth headers? Decisions about exclusions are as important as the SLI formula itself.

### Scale Evolution

#### 1K Users

- **What matters**: You probably do not need formal SLOs. Simple uptime monitoring (Uptime Robot, BetterStack) is sufficient. Focus on shipping features.
- **If you formalize**: One SLO: availability > 99% over 30 days. That permits ~7.2 hours of downtime per month. More than enough buffer for a small team.
- **Tooling**: A free Datadog/New Relic tier or hosted Grafana Cloud (free tier covers 10K active metrics).
- **Error budget**: With ~3 million requests/month at this scale, your 1% budget is 30,000 errors. Plenty of room.

#### 100K Users

- **What changes**: Users are now paying or strategically important. SLA conversations start. You likely have a product manager asking "are we reliable enough?"
- **Add**: Latency SLO alongside availability. Define p99 latency target (e.g., < 500ms). Split SLOs by critical path (checkout, search, read-only browse).
- **Tooling**: Prometheus + Grafana self-hosted, or Datadog at ~$15/host/month. PagerDuty for on-call routing.
- **Key decision**: Define your first SLA. Offer credits for downtime > 1% per month. This forces the team to take reliability seriously.

#### 1M Users

- **What changes**: One engineer cannot know the state of all services. Error budget is now a team coordination tool — product and engineering need shared visibility.
- **Add**: Multi-window burn rate alerting (see Section 2). Separate SLOs per service. Error budget policy documented and signed off by engineering leadership.
- **Tooling**: Thanos or Mimir for long-term Prometheus metrics (>13 months retention). Sloth or Nobl9 for SLO management. Statuspage.io for customer-facing status.
- **Key decision**: SLO ownership. Who owns each SLO? The owning team is paged when the budget burns. No owner = no accountability.

#### 100M Users

- **What changes**: Reliability is now a competitive differentiator. SLA violations cause PR crises, not just refund tickets. Legal/compliance teams review SLAs.
- **Add**: Composite SLOs (a user journey spanning 5 services has one SLO). CUJ (Critical User Journey) mapping. Synthetic probers in every major region emitting SLI data. SLO review in every incident postmortem.
- **Tooling**: Custom SLO platform (Nobl9 enterprise, or bespoke). OpenSLO for cross-vendor metric standardization. Dedicated SRE team owning the error budget policy.
- **Key decision**: SLO tiers. Not all services are equal. Tier 1 (checkout, auth, core API): five nines. Tier 2 (recommendations, notifications): three nines. Tier 3 (analytics dashboards, admin tools): two nines. This tiering determines on-call rotation depth, redundancy investment, and deployment risk tolerance.

## 5. Code / Implementation Perspective

### Python: SLO Tracker (Runnable Pseudocode)

```python
from collections import deque
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from datetime import datetime, timedelta
from typing import Deque, Tuple
import time

@dataclass
class RequestEvent:
    timestamp: datetime
    is_good: bool       # met the SLI definition of "good"
    is_valid: bool      # should count toward total (exclude health checks)


@dataclass
class SLOTracker:
    """
    Tracks a single SLO over a rolling window.
    Thread-safe only if you use one instance per process and batch-write metrics.
    """
    target: float                    # e.g., 0.999 for 99.9%
    window_days: int = 28
    _events: Deque[RequestEvent] = field(default_factory=deque)

    @property
    def window_start(self) -> datetime:
        return datetime.utcnow() - timedelta(days=self.window_days)

    def record(self, is_good: bool, is_valid: bool = True) -> None:
        self._events.append(RequestEvent(
            timestamp=datetime.utcnow(),
            is_good=is_good,
            is_valid=is_valid
        ))
        self._evict_stale()

    def _evict_stale(self) -> None:
        cutoff = self.window_start
        while self._events and self._events[0].timestamp < cutoff:
            self._events.popleft()

    def current_sli(self) -> float:
        valid = [e for e in self._events if e.is_valid]
        if not valid:
            return 1.0
        good = sum(1 for e in valid if e.is_good)
        return good / len(valid)

    def error_budget_remaining(self) -> float:
        """Returns fraction of error budget remaining. 1.0 = full, 0.0 = exhausted."""
        total_budget = 1.0 - self.target    # e.g., 0.001 for 99.9%
        budget_consumed = self.target - self.current_sli()
        if total_budget == 0:
            return 0.0
        remaining = 1.0 - (budget_consumed / total_budget)
        return max(0.0, remaining)

    def burn_rate(self, window_minutes: int) -> float:
        """
        Compute burn rate over a short window relative to a sustainable rate.
        A burn rate of 1.0 means budget depletes exactly at window boundary.
        A burn rate of 14.4 means a 30-day budget depletes in ~50 hours.
        """
        cutoff = datetime.utcnow() - timedelta(minutes=window_minutes)
        recent = [e for e in self._events if e.timestamp >= cutoff and e.is_valid]
        if not recent:
            return 0.0
        bad = sum(1 for e in recent if not e.is_good)
        recent_error_rate = bad / len(recent)
        allowed_error_rate = 1.0 - self.target
        if allowed_error_rate == 0:
            return float('inf')
        return recent_error_rate / allowed_error_rate

    def should_page(self) -> Tuple[bool, str]:
        fast_burn = self.burn_rate(window_minutes=60)
        slow_burn = self.burn_rate(window_minutes=300)  # 5 min

        if fast_burn > 14.4 and slow_burn > 14.4:
            return True, f"P1: burn rate {fast_burn:.1f}x — budget exhausts in <2 days"
        if self.burn_rate(360) > 6.0:
            return True, f"P2: burn rate >6x over 6h — budget exhausts in <5 days"
        return False, ""


# Usage
tracker = SLOTracker(target=0.999, window_days=28)

# Simulate recording requests
for _ in range(9990):
    tracker.record(is_good=True)
for _ in range(10):
    tracker.record(is_good=False)

print(f"SLI: {tracker.current_sli():.4f}")                # 0.9990
print(f"Budget remaining: {tracker.error_budget_remaining():.1%}")  # 100%
print(f"1h burn rate: {tracker.burn_rate(60):.2f}x")
page, reason = tracker.should_page()
print(f"Should page: {page} — {reason}")
```

### Language-Agnostic Algorithm: Multi-Window Burn Rate Alert

```
1. For each SLO defined on service S:
   a. Compute error_rate_1h  = bad_requests(1h)  / total_requests(1h)
   b. Compute error_rate_5m  = bad_requests(5m)  / total_requests(5m)
   c. Compute error_rate_6h  = bad_requests(6h)  / total_requests(6h)
   d. Compute error_rate_30m = bad_requests(30m) / total_requests(30m)

2. Define allowed_error_rate = 1 - SLO_target  (e.g., 0.001 for 99.9%)

3. Compute burn rates:
   burn_1h  = error_rate_1h  / allowed_error_rate
   burn_5m  = error_rate_5m  / allowed_error_rate
   burn_6h  = error_rate_6h  / allowed_error_rate
   burn_30m = error_rate_30m / allowed_error_rate

4. Alert conditions:
   IF burn_1h > 14.4 AND burn_5m > 14.4   → PAGE (P1, critical)
   IF burn_6h > 6.0  AND burn_30m > 6.0   → PAGE (P2, high)
   IF burn_1d > 3.0  AND burn_6h > 3.0    → TICKET (P3, medium)
   IF burn_3d > 1.0                        → WARN (P4, informational)

5. Track cumulative budget consumed:
   budget_consumed = SUM(bad_requests over window) / total_budget_events
   total_budget_events = total_requests(window) * (1 - SLO_target)
```

```yaml
          sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="payment-api",status=~"5.."}[{{.window}}]))
        total_query: |
          sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="payment-api"}[{{.window}}]))
    alerting:
      name: PaymentAPIAvailabilityBurn
      page_alert:
        labels:
          severity: critical
          team: payments
      ticket_alert:
        labels:
          severity: warning
          team: payments

  - name: "requests-latency"
    objective: 99.0
    description: "99% of requests must complete under 300ms"
    sli:
      events:
        error_query: |
          sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_bucket{
            service="payment-api",
            le="0.3"
          }[{{.window}}])) - sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="payment-api"}[{{.window}}]))
        total_query: |
          sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="payment-api"}[{{.window}}]))
```

## 7. Alternatives & Comparisons

| Approach | When to Use | Key Trade-off | Real Adopters |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SLO-based alerting** (multi-window burn rate) | Production services with clear user journeys; teams with error budget policy | Requires SLI definition work upfront; pays off at scale | Google, Netflix, Stripe, Cloudflare |
| **Threshold alerting** (alert if error rate > 1%) | Small teams, simple services, early stage | Fast to set up; generates alert fatigue at scale; misses slow burns | Most startups, legacy enterprises |
| **Uptime monitoring only** (Pingdom, UptimeRobot) | Marketing sites, documentation, low-traffic tools | Zero infra overhead; misses latency degradation and partial failures | Solo projects, SMBs |
| **Composite/CUJ SLOs** (SLO per user journey) | Complex products with multiple user personas | Highest signal quality; significant instrumentation investment | Google, large e-commerce platforms |
| **Budget-based deployment gates** (CI/CD) | Teams that want to encode reliability into their release process | Prevents releases when budget is low; can slow feature velocity during incidents | Spotify, SRE-mature orgs |

### Opinionated Recommendation

**For a team of 5-50 engineers running a production SaaS**: Start with Prometheus + Sloth. Define three SLOs max: availability (99.9%), latency (p99 < 500ms, 99% compliant), and error rate (< 0.1%). Use the multi-window burn rate rules Sloth generates. This setup takes 2 days to implement and gives you 90% of the value of enterprise SLO tooling.

**Do not** start with Nobl9 or a custom SLO platform until you have burned your error budget at least once and your team has felt the pain of tuning SLI thresholds. Buy the tool after you understand the problem.

**Do not** set SLOs without first measuring your current SLI baseline for 4 weeks. Teams consistently set SLOs they are already violating because they guessed targets without looking at data. This poisons trust in the system immediately.

## 8. Trade-offs (Be Opinionated)

### Benefits

- **Quantifies reliability**: Removes the subjectivity from "is this outage bad enough to stop feature work?" The answer is in the budget, not in anyone's opinion.
- **Aligns incentives**: Product managers care about error budget because their feature launches consume it. Engineering and product now share a reliability metric, not adversarial "reliability vs. features" framing.
- **Enables confident deployments**: When the budget is full (>80% remaining), teams can ship aggressively. When it is low (<20%), teams naturally slow down. The budget governs velocity, not fear.
- **Early warning system**: Burn-rate alerting catches problems 48-72 hours before they breach the SLO. Traditional threshold alerting catches problems after they are already catastrophic.
- **Postmortem quality**: Every incident can be quantified: "this incident consumed 23% of our 28-day error budget." That number grounds the postmortem in reality.
- **Justifies reliability investment**: "We need to add a second database replica" is easier to budget when you can show "we are consuming 85% of our error budget on database failover events."

### Costs and Limitations

- **Significant upfront work**: Defining good SLIs takes 2-4 weeks for a non-trivial service. You must instrument correctly, decide what "good" means, and tune thresholds. Teams consistently underestimate this.
- **Measurement gap risk**: You only know what you measure. SLIs built on server-side metrics miss client-side failures, DNS issues, CDN problems. Synthetic probing adds cost and complexity.
- **SLO tuning is ongoing**: The right SLO target changes as usage patterns change. A latency SLO set when your p99 was 200ms is wrong after a new database-heavy feature ships and your p99 is 400ms. Budget churn 3 months from now.
- **Alert fatigue from bad SLIs**: If the SLI is poorly defined (excludes too little, includes noise), burn-rate alerts fire constantly. Teams tune them out. This is worse than no SLO system.
- **Organizational adoption is hard**: The error budget policy requires cross-functional agreement (engineering + product + leadership). In organizations with strong feature velocity culture, freezing features to recover a budget is politically difficult.
- **30-day rolling windows mask spikes**: A catastrophic 2-hour outage consumes roughly 0.3% of a monthly budget (2h / 720h). It shows up as a small blip in the rolling window. Short-window SLOs (7-day) or incident-severity gates are needed alongside rolling windows.

### The Core Tension

**Reliability vs. Velocity.** Every deployment is a risk event that burns budget. Every reliability investment reduces feature throughput. The error budget is the explicit negotiation point between these two forces. Without it, either reliability loses (teams move fast and break things) or velocity loses (teams are paralyzed by the fear of outages). The error budget makes the trade explicit.

### My Recommendation

Set your first SLO at your current measured baseline minus 5%. Not your aspirational target — your current reality. A team violating its SLO on day one has zero credibility with the system. Once you have one quarter of consistently meeting the SLO, tighten it by 0.1% and invest in reliability work to maintain it. This ratchet-and-invest cycle is how Google improved Gmail from four nines to five nines over five years.

Never set five nines as a starting target unless you have already measured that your p99 availability is above 99.999%. You will never hit a target you cannot even see.

## 9. Failure Modes — What Goes Wrong

### Vanity SLOs

- **Trigger**: SLO target is set too low (e.g., 90% availability) so the team never breaches it, or defined in a way that excludes most real failures (e.g., "excluding all 5xx from downstream dependencies").
- **Symptoms**: The SLO dashboard always shows green. The error budget is never exhausted. Real outages are not reflected in the SLO. Users complain while the dashboard shows 100% availability.
- **Detection**: Compare SLO status against customer support ticket volume. If tickets spike during "green" periods, your SLI is wrong. Also: track the percentage of customer-reported incidents that appear in SLO data within 10 minutes.
- **Recovery**: Redefine the SLI from the user's perspective using a top-down approach: "what would a user experience if the service were degraded?" Use synthetic probers (external HTTP checks) as a sanity check on your server-side SLI.
- **Real Example**: Multiple enterprises set SLAs of 99.9% but defined availability as "HTTP endpoint responds with any status code in under 10 seconds" — which made 503s count as "available." Customers complained; dashboards showed green. The SLI definition had to be entirely rebuilt.

---

### Error Budget Ignored (No Policy Enforcement)

- **Trigger**: The error budget policy exists on paper but is not enforced. Feature launches continue even when budget is exhausted. No one is accountable.
- **Symptoms**: Error budget regularly hits 0% but there are no consequences. Teams treat it as a metric to look at, not act on. Recurring incidents of the same type are never fixed.
- **Detection**: Track the time-to-first-action after error budget hits 25% remaining. If no action is taken within 48 hours, the policy is not enforced.
- **Recovery**: Error budget policy enforcement requires executive sponsorship. Engineering VP or CTO must be willing to tell a product team "we are not shipping the next feature until the budget recovers." Without that backing, the policy is decoration.
- **Real Example**: Internal Google postmortems (referenced in the SRE Workbook) describe early implementations of error budgets where teams found creative SLI redefinitions to avoid budget enforcement, rather than fixing reliability issues. The solution was to require SRE sign-off on SLI definition changes.

---

### Alert Storm on Budget Exhaustion

- **Trigger**: SLO alert fires once. Team acks and investigates. The underlying issue is not fixed. Budget continues burning. The same alert fires again in 10 minutes. And again. And again.
- **Symptoms**: On-call engineer is paged 20 times in 2 hours for the same SLO. Alert fatigue sets in. Engineer disables the alert. Real new issues are now silently ignored.
- **Detection**: Track alert-to-acknowledge time. Track duplicate alert rate (same SLO, same cause, within 30 minutes). > 2 duplicates in 1 hour = alert configuration problem.
- **Recovery**: Multi-window burn rate alerting has built-in hysteresis — the fast window (1h) and slow window (5m) must both be elevated. A single spike does not re-fire continuously. Additionally, use PagerDuty's "alert grouping" to suppress duplicate notifications. Most critically: fix the underlying issue, do not ack and ignore.
- **Real Example**: The Cloudflare November 2023 control plane outage revealed that their alerting for control plane SLOs was generating hundreds of pages in parallel because multiple components all had their own SLO alerts. The response to "Code Orange" included alert consolidation and parent/child incident grouping.

---

### Latency SLI Measured Incorrectly

- **Trigger**: Latency SLI measures time from the first byte received by the service to the last byte sent. This misses: network time to reach the service, TLS handshake time, time waiting in the load balancer queue, DNS resolution time.
- **Symptoms**: Server-side latency SLI shows 99.9% of requests < 200ms. Users report the site "feels slow." External synthetic probers report p99 latency of 800ms. There is a 600ms gap.
- **Detection**: Always run external synthetic probers (from multiple regions) alongside server-side metrics. The delta between synthetic prober latency and server-side latency reveals hidden infrastructure overhead.
- **Recovery**: Add CDN and load balancer latency metrics to the SLI. Consider measuring latency at the client side (Real User Monitoring, RUM) if user experience is critical. Client-side RUM is the closest you can get to true user-experienced latency.
- **Real Example**: Stripe's latency incident (documented in post-mortems) showed the gap between server-reported response times and what payment terminals actually experienced. Server metrics showed p99 at 180ms. External monitors showed p99 at 3,400ms. The difference was time spent in TCP backlog queue before the server even accepted the connection.

---

### Wrong SLI for the Business (Good Metric, Bad Question)

- **Trigger**: Team tracks availability and latency SLIs faithfully. The business actually cares about *successful payment conversion rate*. A user might get a 200 response from the API but the payment fails due to a fraud rule bug. SLI says healthy; revenue is crashing.
- **Symptoms**: Business metrics (revenue, conversion rate, signup rate) spike or drop while technical SLOs show green. On-call sees nothing. Finance or product spots it first.
- **Detection**: Track business-level metrics alongside technical SLIs. Map critical business events (payment_created, user_signup_complete, video_play_started) as SLIs, not just HTTP response codes.
- **Recovery**: Add at least one business-outcome SLI to every tier-1 service SLO. For payments: `successful_charges / attempted_charges`. For streaming: `successful_play_starts / play_start_attempts`. These require application-level instrumentation, not just infrastructure metrics.
- **Real Example**: Multiple e-commerce platforms have experienced incidents where the cart and checkout API returned 200 responses but a downstream inventory service bug caused 100% of orders to fail silently. HTTP-based SLOs showed green; business was at a standstill.

## 10. When To Use vs. When To Avoid

### Use SLOs when:

- ✅ **You have real users** whose experience degrades in ways your team is not immediately aware of. SLOs are an early warning system. Without users who can be harmed by degradation, SLOs provide no value.
- ✅ **You have multiple services or teams** where reliability accountability needs to be distributed. SLOs make each team responsible for their own error budget. Shared ownership without SLOs results in blame without accountability.
- ✅ **You are deploying multiple times per week**. High deployment frequency increases the risk of each deployment burning error budget. SLOs gate risky deployments automatically via error budget policy.
- ✅ **Business stakeholders ask "are we reliable?"** SLOs give you a quantitative answer. "We have 73% of our 28-day error budget remaining and are on track to meet our SLO" is a real answer. "I think we're doing okay" is not.
- ✅ **You are preparing to sign enterprise contracts** with uptime commitments. Without a functioning SLO system, you cannot know if you are meeting SLA obligations until you are already in breach.

### Do NOT use SLOs when:

- ❌ **You have fewer than ~1,000 users** or are pre-product-market-fit. The overhead of defining, measuring, and enforcing SLOs is not worth it at this scale. Ship features. Use basic uptime monitoring.
- ❌ **You cannot measure your SLIs accurately yet**. A poorly defined SLI is worse than no SLI — it gives false confidence. If you cannot agree on what "good" means, spend time on that before defining SLOs.
- ❌ **Your organization cannot enforce the error budget policy**. If the response to an exhausted error budget is "we'll deal with it next quarter," do not implement SLOs. The measurement without the policy is theater. Focus on building the organizational culture first.

### The #1 Mistake Engineers Make

⚠️ **Setting the SLO target before measuring the baseline.** Every team does this. They pick 99.9% because it sounds right, set up the SLO, and discover in week one that they are already at 98.7% — immediately in breach. This destroys trust in the system. Always instrument first, run the SLI metric for 4+ weeks, then set the SLO target at `(observed_sli) - 0.5%` as a starting point. You earn the right to tighten the target through reliability work, not by fiat.

## 11. Interview Perspective

### How This Topic Shows Up in Interviews

System design interviews at L5+ at Google, Amazon, Meta, and Netflix routinely include reliability questions. Phrasing you will actually hear:

- "How would you ensure your API meets a 99.9% SLA?"
- "Walk me through how you would set up monitoring for this service."
- "How do you decide when to page on-call vs. just create a ticket?"
- "What metrics would you track for this service, and what thresholds would you set?"
- "If this service degrades, how do you know, and how quickly?"
- "Design an SLO framework for a payment processing service."
- "How do you balance reliability with shipping speed?"

### What the Interviewer Is Actually Evaluating

1. **Do you understand the user impact model?** Interviewers want to see that you define reliability in terms of user experience, not server uptime.
2. **Do you know how to operationalize reliability?** Saying "we need 99.9% uptime" is not impressive. Explaining burn-rate alerting, error budget policies, and the distinction between SLI/SLO/SLA is.
3. **Can you make opinionated trade-offs?** Interviewers will push back on your SLO targets. Can you defend why 99.9% and not 99.99%? Can you articulate the cost of one more nine?
4. **Do you know when SLOs are the wrong tool?** Recommending SLOs for a side project with 100 users shows poor judgment. Saying "at this scale, basic uptime monitoring is sufficient and here's when I would add SLOs" shows good engineering judgment.

### Questions YOU Should Ask the Interviewer

Before answering an SLO design question, ask:

- "What is the user journey we're optimizing for?" (Establishes SLI scope)
- "What is the business cost of downtime — is this a payment path or an analytics dashboard?" (Justifies SLO target)
- "How large is the engineering team, and do we have dedicated on-call rotation?" (Determines alert complexity appropriate)
- "What is the current measured availability baseline?" (Prevents setting an unreachable SLO)

### Answer Examples by Level

**Junior (L3-L4):**
"I would track the error rate and response time of the service using Prometheus. If the error rate goes above 1%, I'd alert on-call. For the SLA, I'd define that the service must be available 99.9% of the time, which allows about 8.7 hours of downtime per year."

*What this shows*: Basic metric awareness, knows uptime math. Missing: SLI/SLO distinction, error budget concept, burn-rate alerting, user-centric SLI definition.

---

**Senior (L5):**
"First, I'd define SLIs that reflect user experience — availability as the ratio of successful requests to total, and p99 latency as a separate SLI. I'd set an SLO of 99.9% availability over a 28-day rolling window and 99% of requests under 300ms. The error budget — 0.1% of requests — becomes the shared metric between product and engineering. Rather than alerting on SLO breach, I'd use multi-window burn-rate alerting: if the 1-hour burn rate exceeds 14.4x, that means we exhaust the budget in 2 days, which triggers a P1 page. I'd use Sloth to generate the Prometheus alerting rules. When the budget drops below 10%, we halt feature deploys and focus on reliability."

*What this adds*: SLI/SLO distinction, error budget mechanics, burn-rate alerting with specific numbers, tool knowledge, feature-freeze policy.

---

**Staff (L6+):**
"Before picking any numbers, I need to know the CUJ — critical user journeys. For a payment API, there are at least three distinct SLOs: charge creation availability (99.99%, because it's revenue-generating), charge creation latency (p99 < 200ms, 99.5% compliant), and webhook delivery availability (99.9% over 24h, because some delay is acceptable). These are separate SLOs with separate error budgets and separate on-call policies.

I'd instrument server-side, but I'd also run synthetic probers from 5 AWS regions that simulate real charge requests — because server-side metrics miss TLS issues, CDN problems, and load balancer queue time. The synthetic prober latency is the SLI we actually alert on.

For the SLO target, I'd measure baseline for 4 weeks and set the initial target at baseline minus 0.5%. Starting at 99.99% without measurement is how you get a team violating their SLO on day one.

The error budget policy requires product VP sign-off. Feature freeze when budget < 20%, reliability sprint when budget < 10%. This policy needs to be a written document, reviewed quarterly. Without organizational enforcement, the technical system is irrelevant.

At 100M+ users, I'd also instrument business-outcome SLIs alongside technical ones — successful_charge_rate, not just HTTP 200 rate. I've seen incidents where HTTP metrics were green but 40% of charges were failing silently due to downstream fraud service issues."

*What this adds*: CUJ decomposition, synthetic probers, business-outcome SLIs, organizational enforcement, scaling judgment, seasoned operational experience.

### Key Numbers to Memorize for Interviews

| Availability % | Nines | Monthly Downtime | Annual Downtime | Typical Use Case |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 99% | Two nines | ~7h 18m | ~3d 15h | Internal tools, dev environments |
| 99.5% | Two and a half | ~3h 39m | ~1d 19h | Non-critical internal services |
| 99.9% | Three nines | ~43m 49s | ~8h 46m | Standard SaaS, most APIs |
| 99.95% | Three and a half | ~21m 54s | ~4h 23m | Business-critical SaaS |
| 99.99% | Four nines | ~4m 22s | ~52m | Payments, auth, core APIs |
| 99.999% | Five nines | ~26s | ~5m 15s | Telco, critical infrastructure |

> **Critical insight**: The cost of each additional nine is roughly **10x** the infrastructure investment of the previous one. Going from three nines to four nines requires active-active multi-region, instant failover, zero-downtime deployments, and 24/7 on-call response under 5 minutes. Going from four to five nines requires all of that plus sub-second failover, geographic redundancy for every dependency, and near-zero-variance deployment tooling. Do not promise five nines unless you have the engineering to back it.

> **Error budget math to memorize**: 99.9% SLO = 0.1% budget = 43m 49s/month. At 1M requests/month, that is 1,000 allowed failures. A burn rate of 14.4x = budget exhausted in 2 days. These numbers come up in design discussions constantly.
