## Problem Statement

Design a video streaming platform where users can upload, transcode, and stream video content globally at the scale of YouTube — supporting 2.5 billion monthly active users, 500+ hours of video uploaded every minute, and billions of views per day delivered with sub-second startup time and seamless quality adaptation.

**Who uses it and at what scale:**
- YouTube: 2.5 billion MAU, 500 hours of video uploaded per minute, 1 billion hours of video watched per day
- Netflix: 270 million subscribers, 3.3 petabytes of video data transferred per day, Open Connect CDN serves 15,000+ ISP partners
- The platform must handle creators (write path) and viewers (read path) — the read:write ratio is approximately 300:1

## Step 1 — Clarify Requirements (5-10 min in interview)

### Functional Requirements

1. **Video Upload**: Users can upload raw video files in any format (MP4, MOV, AVI, MKV, WebM) up to a defined size limit
2. **Video Transcoding**: Uploaded videos are automatically transcoded into multiple resolutions (144p, 360p, 480p, 720p, 1080p, 1440p, 4K) and formats (H.264, VP9, AV1) for cross-device compatibility
3. **Adaptive Bitrate Streaming**: Videos stream via HLS/DASH, dynamically adjusting quality based on the viewer's network conditions and device capability
4. **Video Discovery**: Users can search for videos by title, description, and tags; a recommendation engine surfaces personalized content
5. **Engagement Features**: View counts, likes/dislikes, comments, and subscriptions are tracked and displayed
6. **CDN Delivery**: Video segments are served from edge locations close to the viewer, minimizing latency and buffering

### Non-Functional Requirements

| Requirement | Target |
|---|---|
| Availability | 99.99% (< 52 minutes downtime/year) for streaming; 99.9% for uploads |
| Streaming Startup Latency | < 2 seconds (p99); < 500ms Time-to-First-Byte (TTFB) |
| Transcoding SLA | < 5 minutes for videos under 10 minutes; < 60 minutes for full-length films |
| Consistency | Eventual consistency acceptable for view counts and engagement metrics; strong consistency for upload acknowledgments |
| Durability | 99.999999999% (11 nines) for stored video blobs |
| Throughput | Sustain 1 billion concurrent segment requests at peak |
| Storage Durability | Video blobs replicated 3x across geographically separated regions |

### Out of Scope

- Live streaming / real-time broadcasting (fundamentally different pipeline — RTMP ingest, sub-second latency constraints)
- Digital Rights Management (DRM) deep implementation (Widevine, FairPlay internals)
- Content moderation pipeline and AI-based policy enforcement
- Monetization and ad insertion (CSAI/SSAI complexity)
- Creator analytics dashboard
- Offline download and DRM-protected caching

### Clarifying Questions to Ask the Interviewer

1. **Upload size limits?** — "Are we capping file size at 128 GB (YouTube's limit) or supporting studio-grade RAW files (500 GB+)?" This determines whether we need chunked resumable uploads vs. a simpler single PUT.

2. **Geographic scope?** — "Is this a global platform or regional?" This determines CDN topology — whether we need multi-region origin storage or single-region with global edge distribution.

3. **Latency vs. cost trade-off on transcoding?** — "Does a video need to be available within minutes or hours? Can a creator wait?" Immediate availability requires expensive parallel transcoding workers; batch processing saves 60-80% on compute cost.

4. **Read:write ratio assumptions?** — Helps size CDN vs. origin correctly and informs caching strategy for manifests vs. segments.

5. **Recommendation system required in scope?** — Two-tower retrieval + ranking model is a significant system on its own. Confirming scope prevents going 40 minutes down one rabbit hole.

6. **Mobile-first or desktop-first?** — Mobile clients have lower bandwidth budgets and need aggressive ABR ladder optimization; desktop clients can sustain higher bitrates longer.

7. **What counts as a "view"?** — "Does a 30-second watch of a 10-minute video increment the view counter?" This determines how complex the engagement tracking pipeline needs to be (simple increment vs. Kafka event streaming + aggregation).

## Step 2 — Capacity Estimation (5 min in interview)

### Assumptions

```
Monthly Active Users (MAU):     2,500,000,000  (2.5 billion)
Daily Active Users (DAU):         500,000,000  (500 million, ~20% of MAU)
Videos uploaded per minute:               500  hours of content
Average video size (raw):                  2 GB per hour of footage
Average video duration:                    7 minutes
Read:Write ratio:                        300:1
```

### Write QPS (Uploads)

```
500 hours/min = 500 × 60 seconds = 30,000 seconds of video per minute
Videos uploaded per minute = 30,000s / (7 min × 60s) ≈ 71 videos/minute ≈ 1.2 uploads/second

Peak upload QPS ≈ 10 uploads/second (4x-5x peak factor)
```

### Read QPS (Streaming)

```
DAU:                           500,000,000 users
Average watch time per user:            60 minutes/day
Total watch hours per day:      500M × 60 / 60 = 500,000,000 hours/day
Total watch seconds per day:    500,000,000 × 3,600 = 1.8 × 10^12 seconds/day

HLS segment size:               6 seconds
Segment requests per day:       1.8 × 10^12 / 6 = 300,000,000,000 (300 billion)
Segment requests per second:    300 × 10^9 / 86,400 ≈ 3,472,222/second ≈ 3.5M RPS

Peak segment RPS (3x):          ~10,000,000 RPS (10M RPS)
```

### Storage Estimation

```
Raw video per minute of upload:     500 hours × 60 min/hr × 2 GB/hr = 60,000 GB/min = 60 TB/min
Raw video per day:                  60 TB/min × 60 min × 24 hr = 86,400 TB/day = 86.4 PB/day

Transcoding multiplier:
  - ~6 quality levels × average 40% compression vs raw = 6 × 0.4 = 2.4x
  - Plus thumbnails, audio tracks, subtitles: +0.2x
  - Total stored per raw GB: ~2.6x

Compressed storage per day:         86.4 PB × 2.6 = 224.6 PB/day
Per year:                           224.6 × 365 ≈ 82,000 PB ≈ 82 Exabytes/year
Over 5 years (with deduplication):  ~200 Exabytes (YouTube's actual reported figure: hundreds of EB)
```

### Bandwidth Estimation

```
Ingress (uploads):
  500 hours/min × 2 GB/hr = 1,000 GB/min ≈ 16.7 GB/s ingress

Egress (streaming):
  Average bitrate per stream:  2.5 Mbps (mix of 720p and 1080p)
  Concurrent streams at peak:  ~10,000,000 (10M)
  Total egress:                10M × 2.5 Mbps = 25,000,000 Mbps = 25 Tbps

Netflix self-reported: ~3.3 PB/day egress = 3.3 × 10^15 / 86400 ≈ 38.2 Gbps average, 150+ Tbps peak
```

### Cache Sizing

```
Principle: 20% of videos account for 80% of views (Pareto)
Videos uploaded per day:     71/min × 60 × 24 ≈ 100,000 videos/day
Popular video segments (20%):  20,000 videos × 7 min × 10 segments/min × 4 quality levels
                             = 20,000 × 70 × 4 = 5,600,000 segments
Average segment size:        2 MB (at 2.5 Mbps × 6s)
Cache needed:                5,600,000 × 2 MB = 11.2 TB per edge PoP (manageable)
```

### Server Count Estimate

```
Transcoding workers:
  1 video (7 min, 1 quality level) takes ~7 minutes on 1 core at real-time speed
  Encoding is CPU-bound: ~1:1 real-time for H.264 on modern hardware
  6 quality levels = 6 parallel workers per video
  Videos arriving: 1.2/second → 1.2 × 6 = 7.2 workers needed continuously
  With 3x burst headroom: 22 transcoding workers minimum
  YouTube uses custom VCU chips (20-33x more efficient than software) → fewer physical units

API Servers:
  Assume each API server handles 10,000 RPS
  Non-streaming API (metadata, search): ~100,000 RPS total
  API servers needed: 100,000 / 10,000 = 10 servers (with replication: 30-50)

CDN edge servers:
  CDN is third-party (Akamai, Fastly, CloudFront) or self-hosted (YouTube uses Google's global network)
  Assume 3,500+ edge PoPs globally (Google's actual number)
```

### Component Responsibilities

**Upload API Service**: Handles chunked, resumable uploads (TUS protocol or AWS S3 multipart). Issues presigned PUT URLs so the client uploads directly to object storage, bypassing the API server and eliminating it as a bottleneck. Tracks upload progress and resumes from the last successful chunk on reconnect.

**Raw Video Storage**: Object storage (S3 or GCS) stores the original unprocessed file. This is the source of truth for re-transcoding. Never expose direct URLs — always proxy through CDN with signed tokens.

**Message Queue (Kafka)**: Decouples the upload acknowledgment from the transcoding process. When raw video lands in storage, an event is published. Transcoding workers consume asynchronously. This prevents the upload API from blocking on hours-long transcoding jobs.

**Transcoding Workers / DAG Scheduler**: The most complex subsystem (covered in Step 6). Reads the raw file, splits it into segments, transcodes each resolution in parallel via a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG), and writes HLS segments + manifests back to processed storage.

**Processed Video Storage**: Stores the output HLS/DASH segments and manifests. These are immutable once written and optimized for high-throughput reads. Lifecycle policies delete raw originals after 30 days (keeping processed copies permanently).

**CDN (Content Delivery Network)**: Caches video segments at edge nodes globally. The manifest file (.m3u8) is small and can be cached with a short TTL (1-5 minutes). Individual segment files are large, immutable, and cached with long TTLs (1 year+). Cache hit rate should be >95% for popular content.

**Metadata DB**: Stores video metadata — title, description, upload status, view count, like count, timestamps, transcoding progress. Separated from blob storage because metadata has very different access patterns (random reads, frequent small updates) vs. video blobs (large sequential reads, infrequent writes).

**API Gateway / Video Service**: Handles all client-facing APIs — search, metadata fetch, view count updates, comments. Rate-limited, authenticated, and load-balanced.

**Recommendation Engine**: Two-stage pipeline: (1) candidate generation using a two-tower neural network to retrieve ~1000 candidates from millions of videos; (2) ranking using a heavier scoring model to produce the final ordered list of ~50 videos.

## Step 4 — Data Model

### Core Entities

#### Video Table (MySQL / Spanner for strong consistency on uploads)

```sql
CREATE TABLE videos (
    video_id        VARCHAR(11)   PRIMARY KEY,   -- YouTube-style ID (e.g., "dQw4w9WgXcQ")
    creator_user_id BIGINT        NOT NULL,
    title           VARCHAR(255)  NOT NULL,
    description     TEXT,
    tags            JSON,                         -- ["music", "pop", "2024"]
    category_id     SMALLINT,
    duration_sec    INT,                          -- populated after transcoding completes
    status          ENUM(
                      'UPLOADING',
                      'PROCESSING',
                      'TRANSCODING',
                      'READY',
                      'FAILED',
                      'DELETED'
                    ) NOT NULL DEFAULT 'UPLOADING',
    raw_storage_key VARCHAR(512),                 -- s3://raw-bucket/video_id/original.mp4
    manifest_url    VARCHAR(512),                 -- CDN URL to master .m3u8
    thumbnail_url   VARCHAR(512),
    visibility      ENUM('PUBLIC', 'UNLISTED', 'PRIVATE') DEFAULT 'PUBLIC',
    created_at      TIMESTAMP     NOT NULL DEFAULT NOW(),
    published_at    TIMESTAMP,
    INDEX idx_creator (creator_user_id, published_at DESC),
    INDEX idx_status  (status, created_at)        -- for worker queue monitoring
);
```

#### Video Renditions Table (tracks each transcoded output)

```sql
CREATE TABLE video_renditions (
    rendition_id    BIGINT AUTO_INCREMENT PRIMARY KEY,
    video_id        VARCHAR(11) NOT NULL,
    codec           ENUM('H264', 'VP9', 'AV1'),
    resolution      ENUM('144p','360p','480p','720p','1080p','1440p','2160p'),
    bitrate_kbps    INT,
    segment_count   INT,
    storage_prefix  VARCHAR(512),                 -- s3://processed/video_id/1080p_h264/
    manifest_url    VARCHAR(512),                 -- rendition-specific .m3u8
    file_size_bytes BIGINT,
    created_at      TIMESTAMP,
    INDEX idx_video (video_id, resolution)
);
```

#### View Counts (Bigtable — high write throughput)

```
Row Key Pattern: video_id#YYYYMMDD#hour
Columns:
  views:count         INTEGER  (atomic increments via Bigtable counters)
  views:unique_24h    INTEGER  (HLL sketch for approximate unique viewers)
  engagement:likes    INTEGER
  engagement:dislikes INTEGER
  engagement:comments INTEGER
  engagement:shares   INTEGER
```

**Why Bigtable for view counts?** View counts are the most write-heavy operation in the system. Every stream generates at least one view event. Bigtable handles millions of writes/second with sub-10ms latency. Row key sharding by video_id + date prevents hot-spotting. MySQL would collapse under this write volume.

#### User Subscriptions (Bigtable or Cassandra)

```
Table: subscriptions
Row Key: subscriber_user_id#creator_user_id
Columns:
  sub:created_at    TIMESTAMP
  sub:notify        BOOLEAN

Reverse index:
Table: subscribers_by_creator
Row Key: creator_user_id#subscriber_user_id
```

**Why wide-column?** Fan-out on write (notify all N subscribers when a creator uploads) requires iterating all rows for a given creator_user_id prefix. Bigtable prefix scans are O(n) sequential reads — efficient for this pattern.

#### Comments (MySQL with pagination)

```sql
CREATE TABLE comments (
    comment_id    BIGINT AUTO_INCREMENT PRIMARY KEY,
    video_id      VARCHAR(11) NOT NULL,
    user_id       BIGINT NOT NULL,
    parent_id     BIGINT,               -- NULL for top-level, set for replies
    body          TEXT NOT NULL,
    like_count    INT DEFAULT 0,
    created_at    TIMESTAMP NOT NULL DEFAULT NOW(),
    INDEX idx_video_comments (video_id, created_at DESC),
    INDEX idx_thread (parent_id, created_at ASC)
);
```

## Step 6 — Deep Dive: Transcoding Pipeline

This is the most complex and interview-decisive component. Do not be shallow here.

### Layer 1: The Problem with Naive Transcoding

A naive implementation would transcode one video serially:
```
raw.mp4 → FFmpeg 1080p → FFmpeg 720p → FFmpeg 480p → ... → upload segments
```

This fails at scale for three reasons:
1. **Throughput**: At 1.2 uploads/second, a 7-minute video at 1:1 real-time per quality level takes 6 × 7 = 42 minutes per video. You need 42/1 = 42 workers minimum — and that does not account for bursts.
2. **Long tail**: A 2-hour film takes 12 hours+ to transcode serially. Creators see 12-hour delay before their content is live.
3. **Resource utilization**: Transcoding a single quality at a time leaves all other cores idle.

### Layer 2: DAG-Based Parallel Transcoding Architecture

YouTube and Netflix both use a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) model where transcoding is decomposed into independent tasks that execute in parallel.

```
                           Raw Video
                               │
                    ┌──────────▼──────────┐
                    │   Video Splitter    │  Split into N 2-minute segments
                    │  (Segmenter Node)   │  (GOP-aligned at I-frames)
                    └──────────┬──────────┘
                               │
         ┌─────────────────────┼─────────────────────┐
         │                     │                     │
   ┌─────▼──────┐       ┌──────▼──────┐       ┌─────▼──────┐
   │  Segment 1  │       │  Segment 2  │       │  Segment N  │
   └─────┬───────┘       └──────┬──────┘       └─────┬───────┘
         │                      │                     │
   ┌─────▼───────────────────────────────────────────▼─────┐
   │            Per-Segment Transcoding (in parallel)       │
   │                                                        │
   │   ┌────────┐  ┌────────┐  ┌────────┐  ┌────────┐     │
   │   │  144p  │  │  360p  │  │  720p  │  │ 1080p  │     │
   │   │  H264  │  │  VP9   │  │  VP9   │  │  AV1   │     │
   │   └───┬────┘  └───┬────┘  └───┬────┘  └───┬────┘     │
   └───────┴───────────┴───────────┴────────────┴──────────┘
           │                                    │
   ┌───────▼────────────────────────────────────▼──────────┐
   │                    Segment Merger                      │
   │          Concatenate per-quality segments              │
   │          Generate per-quality .m3u8 playlist          │
   └───────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────┘
                               │
                    ┌──────────▼──────────┐
                    │   Master Manifest   │
                    │  Generator          │
                    │  (master.m3u8)      │
                    └──────────┬──────────┘
                               │
                    ┌──────────▼──────────┐
                    │  Processed Storage  │
                    │  (S3/GCS)           │
                    └──────────┬──────────┘
                               │
                    ┌──────────▼──────────┐
                    │   CDN Warm-up       │
                    │  (prefetch to edge) │
                    └─────────────────────┘
```

**Critical implementation detail — GOP alignment**: When splitting the raw video into segments, cuts must be made at Group of Pictures (GOP) boundaries — specifically at I-frames (keyframes). An I-frame is a full reference frame; P-frames and B-frames are differential. If you split mid-GOP, the decoder cannot reconstruct frames without the prior I-frame. The splitter scans the video container metadata to find I-frame timestamps before splitting. Typical GOP size is 2 seconds for streaming (every 2 seconds = one keyframe), which aligns with the 2-6 second segment duration for HLS.

### Layer 3: Task Scheduling, Worker Architecture, and Failure Handling

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                    Task Orchestrator                        │
│  (Temporal / Apache Airflow / custom DAG scheduler)        │
│                                                             │
│  Workflow state: video_id → [ task_states ]                │
│  Persisted in Postgres/MySQL for durability                │
└──────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────┘
                           │
         ┌─────────────────┼─────────────────┐
         ▼                 ▼                 ▼
   ┌───────────┐    ┌───────────┐    ┌───────────┐
   │ Segment   │    │ Transcode │    │  Merge    │
   │ Task Q    │    │ Task Q    │    │ Task Q    │
   │ (Kafka)   │    │ (Kafka)   │    │ (Kafka)   │
   └─────┬─────┘    └─────┬─────┘    └─────┬─────┘
         │                │                │
   ┌─────▼─────┐    ┌─────▼─────┐    ┌─────▼─────┐
   │ Segment   │    │ Transcode │    │  Merge    │
   │ Workers   │    │ Workers   │    │ Workers   │
   │ (2-5)     │    │ (50-500)  │    │ (5-10)    │
   └───────────┘    └───────────┘    └───────────┘
```

**Worker state machine**:
```
CREATED → SEGMENTING → TRANSCODING → MERGING → MANIFEST_GEN → CDN_WARM → DONE
                                                    ↓ (on any failure)
                                                  FAILED → RETRY (max 3)
```

**At-least-once with idempotency**: Workers use Kafka consumer groups. If a worker crashes mid-transcode, the task stays in the queue (or is re-queued after a visibility timeout). The output file naming convention uses `video_id/segment_num/quality/attempt_num` so partial outputs from a crashed attempt do not corrupt the final merge.

**Real numbers from YouTube's VCU system**: YouTube developed custom Video (trans)Coding Units (VCUs) that achieve 20-33x compute efficiency vs. software on traditional servers. A fleet of VCUs can transcode a 7-minute video into all resolutions in under 2 minutes. Before VCUs, the same job took 40+ minutes.

**Thumbnail generation**: A dedicated DAG node runs FFmpeg frame extraction at timestamp offsets (10%, 25%, 50%, 75% of duration) to generate candidate thumbnails. YouTube's SmartCrop ML model then selects the most visually compelling frame. This runs in parallel with transcoding and does not block the video going live.

**Audio normalization**: Another parallel DAG node runs loudness normalization (EBU R128 / ITU-R BS.1770) to ensure consistent volume across different videos. Output is a separate AAC audio track muxed into each rendition.

### Layer 2: ABR Algorithm — How the Player Decides

The player runs an ABR (Adaptive Bitrate) algorithm continuously. Three major approaches:

**Throughput-based (naive):**
```
estimated_bandwidth = last_segment_bytes / last_segment_download_time
target_bitrate = estimated_bandwidth × safety_factor (0.8)
select rendition where BANDWIDTH ≤ target_bitrate
```
Problem: This is reactive — quality fluctuates rapidly as bandwidth changes, causing visible quality switches.

**Buffer-based (BBA — Netflix's original approach):**
```
if buffer_level < 10 seconds:
    pick lowest quality (prevent rebuffer)
elif buffer_level < 20 seconds:
    maintain current quality
elif buffer_level > 30 seconds:
    step up one quality level
```
Advantage: Stable — won't thrash quality. Disadvantage: Takes longer to reach high quality on fast networks.

**BOLA (Buffer Occupancy based Lyapunov Algorithm — used in modern players):**
```
maximize: Σ [utility(quality_level)] - λ × rebuffer_time
subject to: buffer_level ≥ buffer_minimum
```
This is a control-theory approach that jointly optimizes quality and buffer safety. YouTube's player uses a variant of this.

**Startup heuristic**: The player starts at the lowest quality level to begin playback immediately (low startup latency), then aggressively ramps up quality using the buffer-based algorithm. YouTube starts with the lowest rendition that fits in the first segment download in < 1 second.

### Layer 3: CDN Architecture and Cache Hierarchy

```
Viewer Request
      │
      ▼
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│              Anycast DNS Resolution                         │
│   GeoDNS routes viewer to nearest edge PoP                 │
│   (based on IP geolocation + real-time PoP load)           │
└─────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────────┘
                      │
                      ▼
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│              CDN Edge PoP (L1 Cache)                        │
│   Memory: SSD + RAM cache of hot segments                   │
│   Cache key: URL path (includes video_id + quality + seg#) │
│   TTL: segments = 1 year (immutable); manifests = 60s       │
│   HIT RATE TARGET: >95% for segments of popular videos     │
└─────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────────┘
                      │ MISS
                      ▼
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│              Regional Cache (L2 / Mid-tier)                 │
│   Aggregates misses from multiple edge PoPs in a region     │
│   Reduces origin load by 10-100x compared to L1-only       │
│   Netflix calls these "Regional Open Connect Appliances"    │
└─────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────────┘
                      │ MISS
                      ▼
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│              Origin (S3 / GCS)                              │
│   Only serves 1-5% of total traffic after CDN warm-up      │
│   Multi-region replication for durability                   │
│   Transfer Acceleration for global write performance       │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Cache warming strategy**: After transcoding completes, the system immediately prefetches the first 30 seconds of video segments (the most likely-to-be-watched portion) to the nearest regional cache. For trending or anticipated viral content (e.g., a major creator uploads a video), proactive push to all edge PoPs is triggered before the CDN would naturally populate via organic traffic.

**CDN selection algorithm at the edge**: The player's initial manifest request includes the viewer's IP and a `nearest-server` query. A **steering server** (YouTube's "video server selector") uses:
1. IP-to-PoP latency measurements
2. Real-time PoP capacity utilization
3. Historical download speed from that viewer to each PoP
to return a redirect URL pointing to the optimal edge PoP. This happens in < 50ms.

**Segment URL signing**: All segment URLs include a time-limited HMAC signature:
```
https://cdn.example.com/v/{video_id}/{quality}/seg_{N}.ts?expires={unix_ts}&sig={hmac}
```
This prevents hotlinking and allows per-user access control (e.g., premium-only content). The CDN validates the signature without hitting the origin for every request.

## Step 8 — Scale: Where This Design Breaks

### At 10x Current Load (~10x uploads, ~10x concurrent viewers)

**The bottleneck: Transcoding worker fleet exhaustion**

At 10x upload volume (500 uploads/second), the transcoding queue depth grows faster than workers can drain it. Even with auto-scaling, there is a cold-start lag of 3-5 minutes before new workers are ready.

**Fix**: Pre-warm a standby pool of transcoding workers (20% of peak capacity) during low-traffic hours. Use predictive auto-scaling based on historical upload volume patterns (Monday 9am is a known spike). Move to spot/preemptible instances for 80% of the worker fleet to reduce cost while maintaining throughput.

**Second bottleneck at 10x: Metadata DB write amplification**

10x view events means 10x Bigtable writes. This is manageable — Bigtable handles millions of writes/second — but the aggregation pipeline (Kafka → Flink → Bigtable) becomes a bottleneck if Flink lags behind.

**Fix**: Increase Flink parallelism. Use Kafka partitioning by `video_id % N` to ensure events for the same video land in the same Flink partition, enabling in-memory aggregation before writing to Bigtable.

### At 100x Current Load (~100x everything)

**The bottleneck: CDN origin bandwidth and egress cost**

At 100x viewer load, even with a 95% cache hit rate, the 5% origin requests represent 5x the original total traffic hitting S3/GCS. At 25 Tbps base egress, 100x load = 2.5 Pbps total, with 125 Tbps hitting origin. AWS S3 cannot sustain this from a single region.

**Fix**: Multi-region object storage replication. Store processed video segments in at least 3 geographic regions (US, EU, APAC). CDN edge nodes in each region pull from the nearest origin replica, not a single origin. This reduces cross-ocean latency from 150-200ms to <20ms and distributes origin bandwidth across 3 endpoints.

**Second bottleneck: Search and metadata read latency**

At 100x DAU, the metadata service and Elasticsearch cluster are queried at 100x frequency. Elasticsearch becomes the hot path.

**Fix**: Introduce a read-through cache (Redis) in front of Elasticsearch with a 5-minute TTL for search results on popular queries. Cache video metadata objects aggressively — a video's title, description, and thumbnail URL change rarely after upload. Use a cache-aside pattern: on write (edit video metadata), invalidate the specific video_id cache entry.

### At 1000x Current Load — Fundamental Architecture Changes Required

**What changes at 1000x is not just "add more of the same."**

**1. Transcoding: Move from general-purpose CPUs to custom silicon**

Software-based FFmpeg on commodity CPUs cannot economically scale to 1000x upload volume. YouTube's actual solution: custom Video (trans)Coding Units (VCUs) — purpose-built ASICs for video encode/decode — achieving 20-33x better compute efficiency. At 1000x scale, this is the only economically viable path. The alternative (buying 1000x more CPU instances) would cost billions per year.

**2. Storage: Erasure coding instead of replication**

3x replication of exabytes of video data means 3x storage cost. At 1000x scale, switching from triple replication to Reed-Solomon erasure coding (e.g., 6+3 or 10+4 coding) reduces storage overhead from 3x to 1.5x while maintaining 11-nine durability. This is what Google Colossus and AWS S3 use internally. Trade-off: reconstruction from erasure codes is more CPU-intensive than reading from a full replica.

**3. CDN: Move to ISP-embedded appliances**

At 1000x scale, building and deploying hardware appliances inside ISP data centers (Netflix's Open Connect Appliance model) becomes more cost-effective than paying third-party CDN egress fees. Netflix's OCA program places Netflix-branded servers inside 15,000+ ISPs, achieving >95% of traffic delivered without traversing the internet backbone. The ROI break-even is around 50,000+ subscribers per ISP.

**4. Metadata: Shard the metadata database by region and video_id**

A single global Spanner cluster, even one as powerful as Google's, has cross-region write latency of 70-100ms (speed of light across continents). At 1000x, optimize for write locality: route creators to the nearest regional Spanner cluster, replicate asynchronously to other regions. Accept 5-10 second eventual consistency for global metadata propagation.

## Step 9 — Trade-off Summary

| Decision | Choice Made | Alternative Considered | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upload mechanism | Presigned URL (client → object storage directly) | Upload through API servers | API servers would bottleneck at video file sizes (GBs); presigned URLs keep API servers stateless and cheap |
| Transcoding trigger | Event-driven via Kafka | Polling raw storage for new files | Kafka provides durable, ordered, at-least-once delivery; polling adds latency and wastes CPU when queue is empty |
| Video segmentation | Split raw before transcoding (pre-split) | Transcode full file, then segment | Pre-splitting enables parallel transcoding per segment; reduces end-to-end latency from hours to minutes |
| Streaming protocol | HLS (primary) + DASH (secondary) | Progressive download (MP4 over HTTP) | HLS/DASH enable adaptive bitrate; progressive download cannot switch quality mid-stream |
| Codec priority | AV1 > VP9 > H.264 (fallback) | H.264 only | AV1 achieves 30% better compression at same quality vs H.264; VP9 is the intermediate for unsupported clients |
| View count storage | Bigtable (wide-column, high write throughput) | MySQL with counter columns | MySQL counter updates require row locks; at 3.5M writes/second this serializes writes and causes deadlocks |
| Search index | Elasticsearch | MySQL FULLTEXT index | Elasticsearch provides relevance ranking, fuzzy matching, faceted search, and horizontal scaling that MySQL FULLTEXT cannot match |
| CDN caching | Immutable segments (1-year TTL) + short manifest TTL (60s) | Uniform short TTL for all objects | Segments never change after creation — long TTL maximizes cache hit rate; manifests may update (quality added) so short TTL is safe |
| Transcoding queue | Kafka (durable, partitioned) | RabbitMQ | Kafka's log-based retention allows replaying failed jobs without message loss; RabbitMQ's AMQP model is better for low-volume task queues |
| Recommendation retrieval | Two-tower neural network + ANN search | Collaborative filtering only | Two-tower scales to billions of videos; pure CF requires storing and querying user-item interaction matrix which does not fit in memory at this scale |

## Step 10 — How Real Companies Solved This

### YouTube

**Their actual architecture decisions:**

YouTube runs on Google's global infrastructure — Google Front End (GFE) for load balancing, Colossus for storage, Bigtable for metadata and engagement counters, Spanner for globally consistent transactional data (user accounts, subscriptions), and Borg for job scheduling.

**What they got wrong first — and fixed:**

1. **Early YouTube used MySQL for everything** (2005-2008). As view counts grew, MySQL could not handle the write throughput. YouTube's engineering team wrote a sharding middleware called Vitess (now open-sourced under CNCF) to horizontally shard MySQL across hundreds of nodes. This bought them years before migrating hot tables to Bigtable.

2. **Transcoding was originally a monolithic FFmpeg job** running serially on a single server. A 2-hour film took 12+ hours to transcode. The switch to a DAG-based parallel transcoding pipeline with segment-level parallelism reduced this to under 30 minutes.

3. **CDN origin hot spots**: Early CDN architecture served all traffic from a small number of origin servers. When a video went viral, the origin servers received thundering-herd traffic before the CDN populated. Solution: Add a **coordination layer** — when a video gets more than N concurrent viewers, proactively push its segments to all regional CDN caches before the cache fills organically.

4. **Custom VCU silicon (2023-present)**: YouTube announced their custom Video (trans)Coding Unit chips, achieving 20-33x better compute efficiency than software transcoding on CPUs. This is a multi-billion-dollar infrastructure investment that reduced transcoding cost by approximately 90%.

**Key insight**: YouTube's reliability secret is that video segments are immutable, content-addressed blobs. Once written, they never change. This means any CDN node can serve any segment without coordination — true horizontal scaling with zero consistency overhead.

Source: [YouTube Blog — Reimagining Video Infrastructure](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/new-era-video-infrastructure/)

### Netflix

**Their actual architecture decisions:**

Netflix operates a fundamentally different model from YouTube: it is a closed catalog (Netflix-produced and licensed content only) vs. user-generated content. This changes the economics dramatically — Netflix can precompute optimal encoding parameters for each title because titles are known in advance.

**Per-title encoding (2015, groundbreaking at the time):**

Instead of a fixed "encoding ladder" (same bitrates for all content), Netflix trains an ML model on each video's content complexity. A simple animation (like a cartoon) has little motion and low spatial complexity — it can achieve acceptable quality at 500 Kbps where an action film with explosions needs 3 Mbps for the same perceived quality. Per-title encoding reduced Netflix's average bandwidth consumption by ~20% without visible quality change.

**Per-scene encoding (2018, further evolved):**

Taken further, different scenes within the same film can have different encoding parameters. A dark, simple conversation scene uses fewer bits than a fast-paced battle sequence. Netflix applies Dynamic Optimizer to vary encoding within a single title.

**AV1 at scale (2024):**

Netflix is the largest commercial deployer of AV1 codec, powering ~30% of total viewing hours. AV1 achieves 20-30% bitrate reduction vs. H.264 at the same visual quality. Netflix processes AV1 encodes entirely on AWS (S3 + EC2) as a batch job — each feature film generates hundreds of encoding variants, consuming thousands of CPU-hours per title.

**Open Connect CDN (Netflix's proprietary CDN):**

Netflix does not pay Akamai or Cloudflare. They built Open Connect — a fleet of purpose-built appliances (high-density SSD + large RAM) deployed inside 15,000+ ISP data centers globally. Each OCA serves content to subscribers of that ISP locally, eliminating internet backbone transit. During off-peak hours, OCAs receive proactive pushes of anticipated popular content for the next day (based on the recommendation model's predictions). >95% of Netflix traffic is served from OCAs without traversing the public internet. This reduces both latency and egress cost by orders of magnitude.

**What they got wrong first:**

Netflix originally built on a single Oracle database (2007-2008). When the database failed on a disk corruption event, Netflix was down for 3 days. This catastrophic failure drove the multi-year migration to a distributed microservices architecture on AWS. Today Netflix has no single points of failure — each microservice operates independently with circuit breakers (Hystrix, now replaced by resilience4j).

## Interview Perspective

### What the Interviewer Is Evaluating

For this specific problem, interviewers are looking for:

1. **Do you know what transcoding is and why it is necessary?** (Not just "convert formats" — why multiple resolutions, what is HLS/DASH, what is a codec)
2. **Can you explain the async decoupling between upload and transcoding?** (Presigned URLs, message queue, status polling — not synchronous API blocking)
3. **Do you understand why metadata and blobs are stored separately?** (Different access patterns, different consistency requirements, different scaling strategies)
4. **Can you explain ABR at a meaningful level?** (Not just "the player switches quality" — how does it measure bandwidth, what algorithm drives decisions, how do manifests work)
5. **Do you think about the CDN correctly?** (Not just "put a CDN in front" — cache hierarchy, TTL strategy, cache warming, thundering herd problem)

### Junior (L3-L4) Answer

**What they say**: "Users upload videos, we store them, transcode them into multiple formats, and serve them through a CDN. We use S3 for storage, MySQL for metadata, and maybe Redis for caching. The CDN caches video files close to users."

**What depth they reach**: High-level boxes are correct. They identify the major components. They know HLS exists. They can describe that transcoding produces multiple quality levels.

**What they miss**: How upload chunking works (presigned URLs vs. buffering through API). Why transcoding must be async and the queue decoupling. What a segment is and how the manifest works. The specific performance properties of Bigtable vs. MySQL for view counts. The thundering herd problem when a video goes viral. The cost dimension of storage vs. bandwidth vs. compute.

### Senior (L5) Answer

**What they add**: Presigned URL pattern and why. DAG-based parallel transcoding (segmenting before transcoding). HLS manifest structure and how ABR works. The difference between L1 (edge) and L2 (regional) CDN caches. Bigtable for write-heavy view counts with a justification. Kafka for decoupling upload acknowledgment from transcoding latency. The 95%+ cache hit rate goal and how cache warming achieves it. Status polling vs. webhooks for transcoding completion.

**What differentiates a strong L5**: They call out GOP alignment when discussing segmentation (most candidates don't know this). They discuss the tradeoff between segment size (2s vs. 6s vs. 10s) — smaller segments = faster ABR adaptation but more HTTP overhead; larger segments = smoother streaming but slower adaptation. They discuss codec evolution (H.264 → VP9 → AV1) and why you maintain multiple codec variants.

### Staff (L6+) Answer

**What they drive to unprompted**: Operational concerns that juniors and seniors never reach.

1. **Cost modeling**: "At this scale, compute for transcoding is the dominant cost line, not storage or bandwidth. At 500 hours/minute upload rate, the transcoding bill on commodity EC2 would be ~$X million/month. YouTube's custom VCU silicon justifies its ASIC investment at this volume."

2. **Failure mode analysis**: "What happens if the Kafka consumer group for transcoding falls behind? We need a dead-letter queue (DLQ) and a separate worker that processes DLQ jobs with alerting. What if a video is stuck in TRANSCODING state for 4+ hours? We need a stale-job detector that re-queues or pages on-call."

3. **Global consistency nuance**: "View counts use eventual consistency, but there is a correctness concern with the milestone notifications (e.g., 'Your video hit 1 million views!'). If we count approximately with HyperLogLog, we might miss the milestone or double-trigger it. We need a separate exact counter for milestone tracking on a smaller write path."

4. **CDN cost optimization**: "For videos that are more than 90 days old and have fewer than 1,000 views/month, it is cheaper to evict them from CDN edge caches entirely and serve from origin on demand, rather than paying CDN storage costs for cold content."

5. **Data locality for recommendations**: "The recommendation model needs to be updated in near-real-time as watch patterns change. Serving stale recommendations (24-hour-old model) means missing viral trends. We run model inference on the latest features (last 1 hour of watch events) and serve from a Feature Store (Redis + offline batch) with online feature serving latency < 50ms."

### The 4-5 Mistakes Candidates Make On This Specific Problem

1. **Synchronous transcoding**: Saying "after the user uploads, we transcode and then return the video URL." At any real scale, transcoding takes minutes to hours. The upload API must return immediately with a video_id and PROCESSING status. Synchronous is interview-ending for this problem.

2. **Serving video through API servers**: "The client requests the video from our API servers, which stream the bytes." API servers are stateless request handlers — they must never be in the video byte path. Video bytes go: client → CDN → object storage. Not through application servers.

3. **Misunderstanding CDN caching**: Candidates say "we cache popular videos in Redis" for video content. Redis is not for video bytes (GB per video × millions of videos = not feasible). Redis is for metadata. Video bytes are cached by CDN edge nodes, which have purpose-built SSD arrays optimized for large sequential reads.

4. **Ignoring the thundering-herd problem**: Not discussing what happens when a video goes viral — the CDN is cold (no one has watched it yet), so the first 10,000 concurrent viewers all miss the cache and hit origin simultaneously. This is the most common production failure mode for video platforms. Fix: CDN coalescing (origin shield) and proactive cache warming.

5. **Flat encoding strategy**: "We transcode to 1080p, 720p, 480p, 360p." This ignores that different content has different optimal encoding parameters (per-title/per-scene encoding), that codec choice (AV1 vs H.264) has a 30%+ bandwidth impact, and that you need to maintain backward-compatible renditions for old client versions while adding new codec variants for new clients.
